# Alkanes and Cycloalkanes {#alkanes}

**Alkanes** contain only carbon and hydrogen joined by single bonds. Their names are the parent names for every other organic molecule, so learn them first. Each carbon forms four bonds; in line drawings, every corner and line end is a carbon carrying enough hydrogens to make four bonds.

## Parent names

The first four names (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-) are historical. From five carbons on, the name comes from a Greek or Latin number.

[[alkane-table]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection.** The longer chains in this table are the backbones of the **fatty acids** in your diet and your cell membranes. Palmitic acid (16 carbons) and stearic acid (18 carbons) are the most common saturated fats. You'll meet them in [Biomolecule Connections](#bio).
</div>

## Cycloalkanes

When the carbons form a ring, add **cyclo-** to the front of the alkane name.

## Alkyl groups: the branches

A branch on the parent chain is an **alkyl group**. Name it by changing the alkane's *-ane* ending to *-yl*: methane becomes **methyl**. The R in each drawing marks where the branch attaches to the parent.

[[figs alk_me alk_et alk_pr alk_bu]]

Three-carbon and four-carbon branches can attach in more than one way. Each has a systematic name, which this site uses, and an older common name you'll still see often, especially on labels like *isopropyl alcohol*.

[[figs alk_ipr alk_sbu alk_ibu alk_tbu alk_ph]]

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**Alphabetizing branch names.** Alphabetize by the first letter of the complete branch name: *propan-2-yl* goes under **p**, *2-methylpropyl* under **m**. For the common names, *isopropyl* goes under **i**, but the italic *sec-* and *tert-* are ignored, so *tert*-butyl goes under **b**. A benzene ring used as a branch is called **phenyl**.
</div>

## Naming a branched alkane

1. **Find the longest continuous chain.** It may bend; it doesn't have to run straight across the drawing. If two chains tie for longest, choose the one with more branches.
2. **Number the chain** from the end that gives the branches the lowest numbers, using the first point of difference.
3. **If the numbering still ties**, give the lower number to the branch that comes first in alphabetical order.
4. **Name each branch** with its number. Every branch needs its own number, even two on the same carbon: *4,4-dimethyl*, not *4-dimethyl*.
5. **List branches alphabetically**, then add the parent name.

### Worked examples

[[fig ex_octane]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Longest chain:** eight carbons, so the parent is **octane**. The two-carbon branch on carbon 5 is not part of it; including it would give only seven carbons.
2. **Number:** from the left, the groups sit at {2, 3, 4, 4, 5}. From the right, they'd be at {4, 5, 5, 6, 7}. The first set is lower at the first point of difference (2 vs. 4).
3. **Branches:** chloro at 2, bromo at 3, two methyls at 4, ethyl at 5.
4. **Alphabetize:** **b**romo, **c**hloro, **e**thyl, **m**ethyl (the *di* in *dimethyl* doesn't count).
5. **Name:** **3-bromo-2-chloro-5-ethyl-4,4-dimethyloctane**.
</details>

[[fig ex_heptane]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Longest chain:** seven carbons, **heptane**. The three-carbon branch on carbon 4 is attached at its middle carbon, so it's a propan-2-yl (isopropyl) group.
2. **Number:** from the left, the groups sit at {2, 3, 4}; from the right, {4, 5, 6}. Number from the left.
3. **Alphabetize:** **f**luoro, **m**ethyl, **p**ropan-2-yl. Parentheses go around the complex branch name so it reads clearly.
4. **Name:** **3-fluoro-2-methyl-4-(propan-2-yl)heptane**. With the older branch name, the order changes because *isopropyl* goes under **i**: 3-fluoro-4-isopropyl-2-methylheptane.
</details>

[[fig ex_cyhex]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Parent:** the six-carbon ring, **cyclohexane**.
2. **Number:** the two groups are three positions apart, so either numbering gives {1, 3}. That's a tie.
3. **Break the tie alphabetically:** **b**utan-2-yl comes before **n**itro, so the butan-2-yl carbon is C1.
4. **Name:** **1-(butan-2-yl)-3-nitrocyclohexane**. (Older name: 1-*sec*-butyl-3-nitrocyclohexane. The tie goes the same way, because *sec* is ignored and **b**utyl still comes before **n**itro.)
</details>

### When a molecule has a ring and a chain, or two rings

Under the current rules, a **ring is always the parent over a chain**, and the chain becomes a branch: *propylcyclohexane*, not 1-cyclohexylpropane. (Older rules picked whichever had more carbons, so you may still see names like 1-cyclohexylpropane.) When a molecule has **two rings**, the parent is the ring carrying the highest-priority group. Otherwise, it's the larger ring.

[[fig ex_tworing]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it</summary>

1. **Highest-priority group:** the ketone (C=O), which takes the suffix *-one*. It's on the six-carbon ring, so that ring is the parent: **cyclohexan-1-one**.
2. **Number:** the C=O carbon is C1. Count around the ring the shorter way to the other ring: C3.
3. **The second ring is a branch:** a benzene ring as a substituent is **phenyl**.
4. **Name:** **3-phenylcyclohexan-1-one**.
</details>
