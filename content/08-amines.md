# Amines and Amides {#amines}

Amines and amides both contain nitrogen. In an **amine**, the nitrogen is bonded only to carbons and hydrogens. In an **amide**, the nitrogen is bonded to a C=O carbon. That one difference changes everything about how they behave in the body: amines are bases, and amides are not.

## Amines: –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂

- Replace the parent's final **-e** with **-amine**, and number to give the nitrogen's carbon the lowest number: *propan-1-amine*.
- **Groups on the nitrogen** get the locant **N-** instead of a number: *N-methylethanamine*. Two groups on nitrogen need two N's: *N,N-dimethylethanamine*.
- The parent is the **longest chain bonded to the nitrogen**; the other groups on N become N- prefixes.
- As a prefix, –NH₂ is **amino-**. A nitrogen with two methyls is **dimethylamino-**.
- Common names list the groups on nitrogen alphabetically, then add *amine*: *ethylamine*, *ethylmethylamine*.

<summary>Walk through it: N,N-diethylbut-3-en-2-amine</summary>

1. **Highest-priority group:** the amine, so the suffix is **-amine**.
2. **Parent:** three carbon groups are bonded to the nitrogen: two ethyls and a four-carbon chain. The longest one, the four-carbon chain, is the parent.
3. **Number the parent** so the carbon bonded to N gets the lowest number: C2. The double bond then sits at C3–C4: **but-3-en-2-amine**.
4. **Groups on nitrogen:** two ethyls, so **N,N-diethyl**.
5. **Name:** **N,N-diethylbut-3-en-2-amine**.
</details>

### Primary, secondary, and tertiary amines

Amines are classified differently from alcohols. Count the **carbons bonded directly to the nitrogen**: one makes it **primary (1°)**, two makes it **secondary (2°)**, three makes it **tertiary (3°)**.

[[figs am_p am_s am_t]]

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
**Don't mix up the two systems.** For alcohols, you count carbons on the **carbon** holding the OH. For amines, you count carbons on the **nitrogen**. Butan-2-amine has its NH₂ on a secondary carbon, but the nitrogen has only one carbon, so it's a **primary amine**.
</div>

## Amine salts and drug names

Amines are weak bases. With an acid, the nitrogen picks up H⁺ and becomes a positively charged **ammonium ion**. Systematically, change *-amine* to *-aminium* and name the anion: *ethanaminium chloride*. On drug labels, the same salt is written as the amine plus the acid: *ethylamine hydrochloride*.

[[fig amsalt]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection.** Many drugs are amines sold as **hydrochloride salts**: diphenhydramine HCl (Benadryl), sertraline HCl (Zoloft), lidocaine HCl. The charged salt dissolves in water, which makes it easier to formulate as a tablet or injection. The same chemistry is why fish smells less with lemon juice: acid turns the fishy-smelling amines into odorless salts.

**Review from CHEM&121:** why an N with four bonds carries a +1 charge ([Bonding Patterns and Formal Charge](https://mrkarlb.github.io/clarkcollegeGOBStructures/index.html#patterns)), and why the charged salt dissolves in water ([Charged molecules in the body](https://mrkarlb.github.io/clarkcollegeGOBStructures/shape.html#charged)).
</div>

## Amides: –CO–N

- Replace the parent's final **-e** with **-amide**: *butanamide*. The C=O carbon is C1.
- Groups on the nitrogen get **N-** locants, just like amines: *N-methylbutanamide*.
- An amide on a ring, with the C=O carbon outside it, is named with **-carboxamide**. Benzene is an exception that keeps a common name: **benzamide**.

[[figs amd1 amd2]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: the peptide bond.** When two amino acids join, the COOH of one and the NH₂ of the other form an **amide**. In proteins, this amide link is called a **peptide bond**, and it repeats along every protein chain in your body. Amides also appear in many drugs: **acetaminophen** (Tylenol) and **lidocaine**, the most common local anesthetic in dentistry.
</div>
