# Alkenes, Alkynes, and Cis/Trans {#alkenes}

**Alkenes** contain a carbon–carbon double bond; **alkynes** contain a triple bond. These are the Tier B groups on the ladder: they change the parent's ending, not its suffix.

## Naming alkenes and alkynes

- Change the parent's **-ane** ending to **-ene** (double bond) or **-yne** (triple bond).
- **Number the chain** so the multiple bond gets the lowest number possible. The locant goes right before *-ene* or *-yne*, and gives the lower-numbered carbon of the bond: *but-1-ene* has its double bond between C1 and C2.
- **Two or more multiple bonds:** use *-diene*, *-triene*, *-diyne*, and add an "a" after the parent's root so it can be pronounced: *buta-1,3-diene*, not *but-1,3-diene*.
- **Rings:** number the ring so the double bond runs through C1 and C2. No locant is needed when there's only one: *cyclohexene*.

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**Which chain is the parent?** Under the current rules, the parent is still the **longest chain**, even if it doesn't contain the double or triple bond. Many textbooks give the older rule: choose the longest chain *that contains* the multiple bond. In almost every molecule you'll meet in this course, those are the same chain, so the two rules give the same name.
</div>

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it: both a double and a triple bond</summary>

1. **The double and triple bonds count together** as one set. Number from the end that gives that set the lowest numbers. For **pent-3-en-1-yne**, numbering from the triple-bond end gives {1, 3}; from the other end, {2, 4}. The triple bond is nearer the end, so it gets C1.
2. **If the set ties**, the double bond gets the lower number. For **pent-1-en-4-yne**, both directions give {1, 4}, so the double bond takes C1.
3. **Assemble:** *-en* comes before *-yne* in the name, and the final "e" of *-ene* is dropped before a vowel: pent-1-en-4-yne.
</details>

## Cis and trans

Atoms can't rotate around a double bond the way they can around a single bond. So when each carbon of the double bond has two different groups, the groups can sit on the **same side** (*cis*) or **opposite sides** (*trans*). These are two different molecules, called **stereoisomers**: same atoms, same connections, different arrangement in space.

[[figs ct1 ct2]]

The same idea applies to **rings**, which also can't rotate freely. Two groups on the same face of the ring are *cis*; on opposite faces, *trans*. Wedges point toward you, and dashes point away.

[[figs ct3 ct4]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: trans fats.** Natural unsaturated fats have *cis* double bonds, which put a bend in the chain. Partial hydrogenation of vegetable oil (once used to make margarine and shortening) converted some of them to *trans* double bonds, which keep the chain straight like a saturated fat. Trans fats raise LDL cholesterol and the risk of heart disease, and the US banned added partially hydrogenated oils in 2018. See the fatty acids in [Biomolecule Connections](#bio).
</div>

## E and Z: when cis/trans isn't enough

*Cis* and *trans* only work when you can point to two matching groups. If each carbon of the double bond holds two different atoms, like the molecule below, there's nothing to compare. The **E/Z system** works for every double bond.

1. On **each carbon** of the double bond, rank its two groups by **atomic number**: the atom attached directly to the double-bond carbon with the higher atomic number wins (Br > Cl > O > N > C > H). If they tie, keep going outward along each group until you find a difference. These are the same priority rules used for R and S in [Chirality](#chirality).
2. If the two higher-ranked groups are on the **same side**, the double bond is **Z** (from German *zusammen*, together).
3. If they're on **opposite sides**, it's **E** (*entgegen*, opposite).
4. Write the letter, with its locant, in parentheses at the front of the name: **(2Z)-but-2-ene**.

[[figs ez1 ez2]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it: when cis does not mean Z</summary>

Look at **2-bromobut-2-ene**. The two CH₃ groups are on the same side, so you might call it *cis*. But E/Z ranks by atomic number:

1. **On C2:** Br (atomic number 35) outranks CH₃ (C, 6).
2. **On C3:** CH₃ (C, 6) outranks H (1).
3. **Compare:** Br on C2 and CH₃ on C3 are on **opposite** sides, so this is **(2E)-2-bromobut-2-ene**.

*Cis*/*trans* and E/Z are two different systems. They often agree, but not always, so don't translate one into the other.
</details>
