# Alcohols, Ethers, Halides, Nitro Compounds, and Thiols {#alcohols}

This section covers single-bonded groups containing oxygen, halogens, nitrogen, and sulfur. Alcohols and thiols are Tier A groups that can take the suffix; ethers, halogens, and nitro groups are Tier C and are always prefixes.

## Alcohols: –OH

- Replace the final **-e** of the parent with **-ol**, and number the chain so the OH carbon gets the lowest number: *propan-2-ol*.
- Two or three OH groups: keep the *-e* and add *-diol* or *-triol*: *ethane-1,2-diol*.
- When a higher-priority group takes the suffix, OH becomes the prefix **hydroxy-**.
- The locant "1" is dropped only when there's no other locant in the name and no doubt where the OH is: *ethanol*, *cyclohexanol*.

[[figs alc1 alc2 alc3]]

### Primary, secondary, and tertiary alcohols {#alcohol-class}

Classify an alcohol by counting the **carbons bonded to the carbon that holds the OH**: one carbon makes it **primary (1°)**, two makes it **secondary (2°)**, three makes it **tertiary (3°)**. The class predicts how the alcohol reacts, for example whether it can be oxidized to an aldehyde or ketone.

[[figs alc_p alc_s alc_t]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection.** Propan-2-ol is **isopropyl alcohol**, the rubbing alcohol in alcohol prep pads. Molecules with several OH groups are common in the body and in medicine: **ethylene glycol** (antifreeze, a dangerous poisoning), **glycerol** (the backbone of fats), and **xylitol**, a sugar substitute in sugar-free gum that oral bacteria can't ferment, which helps reduce tooth decay.
</div>

[[figs diol triol xylitol]]

## Ethers: C–O–C

An ether oxygen joins two carbon groups. In the current rules, the larger group is the parent and the smaller is an **alkoxy-** prefix (methoxy, ethoxy). The older common names, still widely used, list both groups alphabetically and add the word *ether*.

[[figs ether1 ether2]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection.** Ethoxyethane is **diethyl ether**, the first widely used surgical anesthetic (1846). Modern inhaled anesthetics such as sevoflurane are halogenated ethers.
</div>

## Alkyl halides: C–F, C–Cl, C–Br, C–I

Halogens are always prefixes: **fluoro-**, **chloro-**, **bromo-**, **iodo-**. Number and alphabetize them like any branch. Simple ones often keep common names that name the alkyl group, then the halide: *ethyl chloride*.

[[figs halo1 halo2 halo3]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection.** Chloroethane (**ethyl chloride**) is a cold spray that numbs skin before injections. Halothane was a widely used inhaled anesthetic. Many environmental pollutants are halogenated too, such as PCBs and some pesticides, because carbon–halogen bonds make molecules last a long time in nature.
</div>

## Nitro compounds: C–NO₂

Like halogens, the nitro group is **always a prefix**: **nitro-**. It never names the parent.

[[fig nitro_ex]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health and environment connection.** You don't need to name these molecules, but you'll recognize the nitro group in them. **Nitrofurantoin** (Macrobid) is one of the most prescribed antibiotics for urinary tract infections. **Metronidazole** (Flagyl) treats many infections, including dental and gum infections. **TNT** (2-methyl-1,3,5-trinitrobenzene) is an explosive that contaminates soil and groundwater at military sites, and is a target of bioremediation.
</div>

[[figs nitro_furan nitro_metro nitro_tnt]]

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
**Watch out: nitroglycerin is not a nitro compound.** Nitroglycerin, a heart medication for chest pain (angina), has its NO₂ groups attached to **oxygen**, not carbon. That makes it a **nitrate ester**, with the systematic name *propane-1,2,3-triyl trinitrate*. A nitro compound needs C–NO₂.
</div>

[[fig nitro_ntg]]

## Thiols and disulfides: –SH and S–S

Thiols are the sulfur versions of alcohols. Add **-thiol** to the full alkane name, keeping the final *-e*: *ethanethiol*. As a prefix, –SH is **sulfanyl-**. Two thiols can link through their sulfur atoms to form a **disulfide**, R–S–S–R.

[[figs thiol1 thiol2 disulf]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection.** Thiols have strong odors: ethanethiol is added to natural gas so leaks can be smelled, and sulfur compounds cause bad breath. In proteins, the amino acid **cysteine** has a thiol, and two cysteines link into a **disulfide bridge** that holds the protein's shape. Hair perms work by breaking and re-forming these bridges. **Cysteamine** is a medication for cystinosis, a rare disease in which cystine builds up in cells.
</div>
