# Aromatic Compounds {#aromatics}

**Benzene** is a six-carbon ring drawn with three alternating double bonds. Those electrons are actually shared evenly around the whole ring, which makes benzene unusually stable: it doesn't react like an alkene. Compounds containing a benzene ring are called **aromatic**. Benzene rings appear in a huge share of medicines, from aspirin and acetaminophen to most antidepressants.

[[figs ar_benz ar_naph]]

## One group on the ring

**Most groups are named as a prefix on "benzene,"** with no number needed, since every position on the ring is the same.

[[figs ar_cl ar_no2 ar_et]]

**Six common parent names are kept by IUPAC**, and you'll see them constantly. Learn these by sight.

[[figs ar_tol ar_phol ar_anil ar_anis ar_bzac ar_bzal]]

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**Toluene keeps its name only on its own.** When a ring has a methyl group plus anything else, the current rules name it as a substituted benzene: *2,4,6-trinitrotoluene* (TNT) is *2-methyl-1,3,5-trinitrobenzene*. The common name is still everywhere, so recognize both.
</div>

When a carbonyl sits **between** a benzene ring and a chain, the chain is the parent and the ring is a **phenyl** branch. The common names end in *-phenone*.

[[figs ar_acph ar_bzph]]

## Benzene as a branch: phenyl and benzyl

A benzene ring attached as a branch is **phenyl** (C₆H₅–). Don't confuse it with **benzyl**, which is a benzene ring plus one CH₂ (C₆H₅CH₂–). Benzyl alcohol, a preservative in many injectable medicines, is C₆H₅CH₂OH.

[[figs alk_ph ar_bn]]

## Two groups: numbers, or ortho, meta, para

With two groups on the ring, their relative positions matter. The current rules use numbers; you'll still see the older letter prefixes everywhere, especially in drug and chemical names.

[[figs ar_o ar_m ar_p]]

| Positions | Number style | Older letter style |
|---|---|---|
| next to each other | 1,2- | *ortho-* (*o-*) |
| one carbon between | 1,3- | *meta-* (*m-*) |
| across the ring | 1,4- | *para-* (*p-*) |

## Naming a substituted benzene

1. **Is one of the groups a parent from the list above** (phenol, aniline, anisole, benzoic acid, benzaldehyde)? If so, use that parent name, and **its carbon is C1**. If two of them are present, the one higher on the [priority ladder](#ladder) is the parent; the other becomes a prefix.
2. **If not, the parent is benzene.** Number the ring to give the groups the lowest numbers.
3. **If the numbers tie**, the group first in alphabetical order gets C1.
4. **List the prefixes alphabetically** with their numbers, then the parent.

[[figs ar_ex1 ar_ex2 ar_ex3]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it: 3-aminobenzoic acid</summary>

1. **Two parent-type groups:** an NH₂ (aniline) and a COOH (benzoic acid). The carboxylic acid is higher on the ladder, so the parent is **benzoic acid**, and the COOH carbon is C1.
2. **Number toward the other group:** counting the short way around the ring, the NH₂ is on C3.
3. **The amine becomes a prefix:** **amino-** (see the [priority ladder](#ladder)).
4. **Name:** **3-aminobenzoic acid**, or in letter style, *m*-aminobenzoic acid.
</details>

## Three or more groups

Letter prefixes only work for two groups. With three or more, always use numbers.

[[figs ar_ex4 ar_tnt]]

<details class="walk" markdown="1">
<summary>Walk through it: 3,4-dichloro-N-methylaniline</summary>

1. **Parent:** the nitrogen group makes this an **aniline**, so the nitrogen's ring carbon is C1.
2. **Number the ring** to give the two chlorines the lowest numbers: {3, 4} beats {4, 5}.
3. **The methyl is on the nitrogen**, not the ring, so its locant is ***N*-** instead of a number. You'll use *N-* again in [Amines and Amides](#amines).
4. **Alphabetize:** **c**hloro before **m**ethyl.
5. **Name:** **3,4-dichloro-*N*-methylaniline**.
</details>

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: antioxidants, sunscreens, and dental care.** Phenols trap the free radicals that damage cells and spoil food: **BHT** is a phenol antioxidant added to cereals and cosmetics. Aromatic rings absorb ultraviolet light, which is how sunscreen filters such as **oxybenzone** work; Hawaii banned oxybenzone sunscreens in 2021 over concerns about harm to coral reefs. **Eugenol**, the main compound in clove oil, is mixed with zinc oxide to make temporary dental fillings and cements. The topical anesthetic **benzocaine** (in [Esters](#acids)) is a benzene ring with an amino group and an ester.
</div>

[[figs ar_bht ar_oxy ar_eug]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Environment connection.** **1,4-Dichlorobenzene** (*para*-dichlorobenzene) and **naphthalene** are the chemicals in mothballs and toilet deodorizer blocks, and both are household air pollutants. **TNT** contaminates soil and groundwater at military sites and is a target of bioremediation.
</div>
