# Start Here {#start}

Your body runs thousands of chemical reactions, but almost all of them are a handful of types, and **enzymes run those types in pairs**: one reaction, and the reaction that undoes it.

| The pair | What changes | The enzymes that run it |
|---|---|---|
| **[Oxidation and reduction](#redox)** | Hydrogen atoms are removed or added (carried by NAD⁺ or FAD) | dehydrogenases and reductases (*oxidoreductases*) |
| **[Addition and elimination](#addelim)** | Two pieces add across a double bond, or leave to form one | hydratases and dehydratases (*lyases*) |
| **[Condensation and hydrolysis](#condhyd)** | Two molecules join by releasing water, or water splits them apart | ligases build, using energy from ATP; digestive enzymes split (*hydrolases*) |

The goal of this page is to let you **look at a reaction and name its type**, from two things: **which functional group reacts**, and **which group forms**. Once you can name the type, you also know its partner, and you'll recognize both when they show up again in proteins, enzymes, and metabolism.

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**Before you start.** You'll need to recognize the functional groups by sight: alkene, alcohol, aldehyde, ketone, carboxylic acid, ester, amine, and amide. If any are shaky, review them in the [naming guide](./#ladder) and your Chemfolio first.
</div>

## How to read a reaction {#read}

- **Starting materials** are on the left, **products** on the right.
- **Above or below the arrow** is what makes the reaction go: a catalyst (H₃O⁺, a metal), heat, or in the body, an **enzyme**. Anything written there is not used up.
- **A double arrow (⇌)** means the reaction runs both ways. On this page, a double arrow labeled on both sides shows a **pair**: the label on top is the forward reaction, the label underneath is the reverse.
- **"2 H"** in a product means two hydrogen atoms were removed. In the body they don't float free: a coenzyme picks them up. NAD⁺ becomes NADH + H⁺, or FAD becomes FADH₂.

This page doesn't cover **mechanisms** (how electrons move during a reaction). You need to know what goes in and what comes out.

## Identify the reaction type {#identify}

Find the functional group that changes, then find it in the left column.

| What reacts | What forms | Reaction type | Its partner |
|---|---|---|---|
| alkene (C=C) + water | alcohol | **addition** (hydration) | elimination (dehydration) |
| alcohol | alkene (C=C) + water | **elimination** (dehydration) | addition (hydration) |
| alkene (C=C) + H₂, X₂, or HX | alkane, or a halide | **addition** | — |
| aldehyde or ketone + alcohol | hemiacetal (sugar rings) | **addition** | elimination (ring opening) |
| 1° alcohol | aldehyde, then carboxylic acid | **oxidation** (loses H) | reduction |
| 2° alcohol | ketone | **oxidation** (loses H) | reduction |
| aldehyde or ketone | alcohol | **reduction** (gains H) | oxidation |
| –CH₂–CH₂– | –CH=CH– (C=C) | **oxidation** (loses H, to FAD) | reduction |
| carboxylic acid + alcohol | ester + water | **condensation** | hydrolysis |
| carboxylic acid + amine | amide + water | **condensation** | hydrolysis |
| sugar + sugar | glycosidic bond + water | **condensation** | hydrolysis |
| ester, amide, or glycoside + water | the two pieces | **hydrolysis** | condensation |
| carboxylic acid, or amine | carboxylate, or ammonium ion | **[acid–base](#acidbase)** | acid–base |

## Three questions that sort most reactions

1. **Did the molecule lose or gain H, with no water involved?** Losing 2 H is **oxidation**; gaining 2 H is **reduction**. Look for NAD⁺, NADH, FAD, or FADH₂ near the arrow.
2. **Did a double bond appear or disappear, while the carbon skeleton stayed in one piece?** Two pieces (H and OH, for example) adding across a double bond is **addition**; two pieces leaving to form a double bond is **elimination**.
3. **Did two molecules join, or one split?** Joining with water released is **condensation**; splitting with water used up is **hydrolysis**.

<div class="warn" markdown="1">
**Water shows up in two pairs.** In **addition and elimination**, water adds across a C=C (or leaves to form one), and the carbon skeleton stays in one piece. In **condensation and hydrolysis**, water is released when two molecules join, or used up when one molecule is split into two. Ask whether the number of carbon pieces changed.
</div>
