# Condensation and Hydrolysis {#condhyd}

- **Condensation:** two molecules **join**, and a small molecule, usually **water**, is released. An OH comes from one molecule and an H from the other.
- **Hydrolysis** ("splitting with water"): **water is used up** to break one molecule into two. The OH from water goes to one piece and the H to the other.

Condensation builds the large molecules of life: fats, proteins, and carbohydrates. Hydrolysis takes them apart, which is what digestion is. Enzymes that run hydrolysis are called **hydrolases**, and their names usually say which bond they split: lip**ase** (fats), pept**idase** (proteins), lact**ase** (lactose).

## The pair: esters {#esters}

A carboxylic acid and an alcohol condense to an **ester** and water; water hydrolyzes the ester back into the acid and the alcohol.

[[rxn pr_condhyd]]

**To find the pieces of any ester,** split it at the single C–O bond next to the C=O. The side with the C=O becomes the carboxylic acid (add OH); the other side becomes the alcohol (add H). The same move works on amides (split the C–N bond) and on the glycosidic bonds between sugars.

## Building molecules: condensation {#condensation}

**Amides** form from a carboxylic acid and an amine, with water released. (At room temperature, an acid and an amine only trade a proton; see [acid–base reactions](#acidbase). Forming the amide takes heat in the lab, or an enzyme in the body.)

[[rxn r_amide]]

When the acid and the amine are both amino acids, the new amide is a **peptide bond**. A protein is hundreds of amino acids joined this way.

[[rxn pr_pep]]

**Triglycerides** (fats and oils) form when each of glycerol's three OH groups condenses with a fatty acid, releasing three waters:

[[rxn r_tg]]

**Glycosidic bonds** join sugars. The OH on carbon 1 of one sugar condenses with an OH on another sugar, releasing water. Maltose is two glucose units joined from carbon 1 of the first to carbon 4 of the second, with the first glucose in its α form:

[[rxn r_malt]]

<div class="note" markdown="1">
**In your cells, building takes energy.** Left alone, condensation in water runs backward: water hydrolyzes the bond. So cells don't condense two molecules directly. They first attach one of them to a phosphate from ATP (or a similar carrier), then join the pieces. The enzymes that build this way are called **ligases** (or synthetases). The overall change is the same as the condensations shown here: two molecules joined, and the elements of water removed.
</div>

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: lactose intolerance.** Most adults make less lactase than they did as children. Lactose that isn't hydrolyzed passes into the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, causing gas, bloating, and diarrhea. Lactase tablets supply the missing enzyme, and lactose-free milk has had its lactose hydrolyzed before it's sold.
</div>

Hydrolysis also happens outside the body. Aspirin is an ester, and moisture slowly hydrolyzes it:

[[rxn r_asp]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: old aspirin.** One product is acetic acid, the acid in vinegar. A bottle of aspirin that smells like vinegar has started to break down and should be thrown away. In the body, the same hydrolysis happens on purpose: aspirin is hydrolyzed in the blood within minutes, and much of its pain relief comes from the salicylate it releases.
</div>

## Hydrolysis with a base: saponification {#saponification}

Hydrolyzing an ester with a strong base, such as sodium hydroxide, gives the **carboxylate** ion instead of the acid. Hydrolyzing a fat this way gives glycerol and three fatty acid carboxylates, which are **soap**. The name comes from the Latin word for soap.

[[rxn r_sapon]]

Soap works because each carboxylate has an ionic end that dissolves in water and a long hydrocarbon tail that dissolves in grease.
