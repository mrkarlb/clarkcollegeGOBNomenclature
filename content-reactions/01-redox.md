# Oxidation and Reduction {#redox}

In organic chemistry and in your cells, the simplest way to track oxidation is by hydrogen:

- **Oxidation:** a molecule **loses 2 H**: one from a carbon and one from the atom next to it. The bond between those two atoms becomes a double bond.
- **Reduction:** a molecule **gains 2 H**, and a double bond becomes a single bond.

Gaining an oxygen also counts as oxidation, which is why an aldehyde becoming a carboxylic acid is an oxidation. Each step along this row is an oxidation; each step back is a reduction.

[[oxladder methane meoh methanal formic co2]]

<p class="mapnote">Moving right, the carbon has fewer bonds to H and more bonds to O. Carbon dioxide is as oxidized as carbon gets, and it's what your cells make from the carbons in food.</p>

## The pair: one reaction, two directions {#redox-pair}

The same enzyme can often run the reaction either way. Removing 2 H from ethanol gives ethanal; adding 2 H back gives ethanol.

[[rxn pr_redox]]

In the body, the 2 H are carried by a **coenzyme**, a small helper molecule that works with the enzyme:

- **NAD⁺** picks up 2 H (as NADH + H⁺) when a **C–O** becomes a **C=O**: alcohol to aldehyde or ketone.
- **FAD** picks up 2 H (as FADH₂) when a **C–C** becomes a **C=C**.
- **NADH** and **NADPH** give 2 H back when the body needs a reduction.

Enzymes that run these reactions are named for it: **dehydrogenases** remove hydrogen, **reductases** add it. Both belong to the enzyme class called **oxidoreductases**.

## Oxidizing alcohols: 1°, 2°, and 3° {#alcohols}

Whether an alcohol can be oxidized, and what it becomes, depends on how many H atoms are on the carbon holding the OH.

**A 1° alcohol** oxidizes to an **aldehyde**, and the aldehyde oxidizes again to a **carboxylic acid**. Your liver does exactly this to ethanol:

[[rxn r_etoh1]]

[[rxn r_etoh2]]

**A 2° alcohol** oxidizes to a **ketone** and stops: the carbonyl carbon has no H left to lose.

[[rxn r_ipa]]

**A 3° alcohol** doesn't oxidize: the carbon holding the OH has no H at all.

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: alcohol poisoning is an oxidation problem.**

- **Ethanol:** the aldehyde in the middle, ethanal (acetaldehyde), causes the flushing, nausea, and headache of a hangover. The drug disulfiram, used to treat alcohol use disorder, blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase, so ethanal builds up and drinking makes the patient sick. A common genetic variant of the same enzyme causes facial flushing after one drink.
- **Methanol:** the same two enzymes turn it into methanal (formaldehyde) and then formic acid, which causes dangerous acidosis and can damage the optic nerve and cause blindness. The antidote, fomepizole, blocks alcohol dehydrogenase so the methanol is excreted before it's oxidized.
- **Isopropyl alcohol** (rubbing alcohol), a 2° alcohol, is oxidized to acetone, which patients exhale: a fruity, nail-polish smell on the breath.
</div>

## Reduction: adding the 2 H back {#reduction}

Reduction turns an **aldehyde or ketone** back into an **alcohol**: an aldehyde becomes a 1° alcohol, a ketone a 2° alcohol.

In a working muscle that's short on oxygen, lactate dehydrogenase reduces pyruvate to lactate, which regenerates NAD⁺ so the muscle can keep making ATP. When oxygen returns, the liver and heart run the same reaction the other way.

[[rxn pr_ldh]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection.** Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) leaks out of damaged cells, so a high **LDH** level on a blood panel is a general sign of tissue damage, and high blood **lactate** is a warning sign in sepsis and shock, when tissues aren't getting enough oxygen.
</div>

## Sugars: oxidation and reduction {#sugar-redox}

A sugar's open-chain form has an **aldehyde**, so it can be oxidized (to a carboxylic acid) or reduced (to an alcohol), just like ethanal.

[[rxn r_glcox]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: testing for glucose.** Urine and blood glucose test strips contain the enzyme glucose oxidase. It oxidizes glucose and hands the 2 H to oxygen, making hydrogen peroxide, which a second enzyme uses to turn a dye a color. The deeper the color, the more glucose.
</div>

[[rxn r_sorb]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: sugar alcohols.** Reducing glucose's aldehyde gives **sorbitol**, a sugar alcohol used in sugar-free gum and candy. In uncontrolled diabetes, high blood glucose drives this reaction inside the lens of the eye, and the sorbitol that builds up there contributes to cataracts.
</div>
