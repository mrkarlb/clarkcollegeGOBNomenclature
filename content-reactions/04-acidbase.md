# Acid–Base Reactions {#acidbase}

An acid–base reaction moves **one H⁺** (a proton) from an acid to a base. No carbon bonds change, so it isn't one of the three pairs, but it decides what form every acid and amine takes in your body. That's why the metabolism molecules on this page are drawn as ions: lactate, not lactic acid; fumarate, not fumaric acid.

## Carboxylic acids give up H⁺ {#acids}

A carboxylic acid is a weak acid: in water, it gives its H⁺ to water, forming a **carboxylate** ion and hydronium. The double arrow shows the equilibrium.

[[rxn r_acidw]]

At the pH of blood (about 7.4), the equilibrium lies far to the right: nearly every carboxylic acid in your body is a carboxylate, named with **-ate**. You'll see "lactic acid" and "lactate" used for the same thing, in your textbook and in the news; in the body, nearly all of it is lactate.

## Amines pick up H⁺ {#amines}

An amine is a weak base. The lone pair on nitrogen picks up an H⁺, forming an **ammonium ion**. With hydrochloric acid, the product is an ammonium chloride salt:

[[rxn r_dph]]

<div class="health" markdown="1">
**Health connection: "HCl" on a drug label.** Many drugs are amines, and amines with large carbon groups don't dissolve well in water. As ammonium salts they do, which is why drug labels list names like *diphenhydramine hydrochloride*. Once in the body, the salt and the free amine are in equilibrium, just like the reactions above.
</div>

## An acid and an amine together {#acid-amine}

Mixed at room temperature, a carboxylic acid and an amine simply trade a proton and form a salt. This is **not** an amide: no water is lost and no C–N bond forms. Making the amide takes heat or an enzyme (see [condensation](#condensation)).

[[rxn r_acidam]]

## Amino acids: both at once {#zwitterions}

An amino acid has a carboxylic acid **and** an amine in the same molecule. Near pH 7, the acid gives up its H⁺ and the amine picks one up, so the molecule carries both a positive and a negative charge, with no overall charge: a **zwitterion**. This is the form amino acids take in your blood and cells.
